# Triangulate UK r* instead of trusting HLW

The dashboard's UK r* of **−0.8% ± 1.1 (2025Q4)** is not a finding about the UK economy. It comes from a model that the New York Fed itself stopped running for the UK in 2023 because it had become "highly unreliable". Several of the dashboard's specification choices then push that model's known downward biases further. Best practice has four parts. First, repair HLW with Buncic's corrected median-unbiased estimation (MUE), the HLW-2023 COVID treatment, an expectations-based and ELB-aware real rate, and full identification diagnostics. Second, demote HLW to one member of a small **suite**: a Del Negro et al.-style trend-cycle model on gilt yields and inflation expectations, the BoE Market Participants Survey (MaPS) neutral rate, a clearly labelled market-implied real forward, and simple moving-average and global benchmarks. Third, publish a **rounded headline and a range by method class**, as the Bank of England, ECB, Bank of Canada and Norges Bank all do. Fourth, check the result against published benchmarks: the MPC's Alan Taylor puts UK real r* at **about 0.75% (2025Q1, 95% band roughly 0.25–1.25%)**, and the September 2026 MaPS median neutral Bank Rate is **3.25% nominal (≈1.25% real)**. A rebuilt suite should land at roughly **0.5–1.25% real (2.5–3.25% nominal)**. With Bank Rate at 3.75%, the stance would then read "modestly restrictive / near the top of neutral" rather than "tight – room to ease". That is consistent with the Bank's own February 2026 estimate of a real-rate gap of around 0–1 pp.

## The −0.8% is a known HLW failure mode, not a finding

The dashboard model follows Holston, Laubach and Williams (HLW). HLW is a Kalman-filter model with an IS curve and a Phillips curve. Three random-walk latent states (potential output y*, trend growth g, and a residual "other" factor z) combine as **r* = c·g + z**, and two signal-to-noise ratios (λg, λz) are pinned by Stock–Watson median-unbiased estimation because maximum likelihood piles up at zero ([HLW 2023, SR1063](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1063.pdf)). The UK problem is documented. In SR1063 the NY Fed wrote that extending the sample "has weakened the estimated relationship between the output gap and real interest rates in the UK data, making estimates of the natural rate of interest highly unreliable. For that reason, we no longer estimate the model for the United Kingdom" ([SR1063](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1063.pdf)). John Williams added that UK estimates "were highly imprecise" even before the pandemic ([Williams, May 2023](https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/speeches/2023/wil230519)). The NY Fed now publishes HLW only for the US, Canada and the euro area ([NY Fed r*](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/policy/rstar)). The Bank of England's own staff suite does not name HLW among its models ([BoE MPR Feb 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2025.pdf)).

Four features of the current specification each lean the estimate down. **First, λz = 0.03 lets z drift.** Buncic shows that HLW's Stage-2 MUE is misspecified. It adds an unsupported constant, leaves the g loading unrestricted, and puts extra regressors inside the break tests, and all three inflate λz. Once corrected, **λz = 0 exactly for the UK**, z "is essentially a horizontal line at 0", and **UK r* at 2019Q4 rises from 1.35% to 1.80%** ([Buncic, arXiv 2103.16452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16452)). A λz of 0.03 therefore gives z freedom that the corrected UK data do not support, and a random-walk z keeps whatever downward shifts the 2009–2023 period imposes. **Second, the real rate ignores the ELB.** From 2009 to 2021 Bank Rate sat at 0.1–0.5% while QE loosened conditions further. Treating those observations as the true policy stance forces the IS curve to explain a real-rate gap that it cannot see. Johannsen and Mertens show that "ignoring the ELB (treating zero as data) yields a lower trend nominal rate", because the model must explain the flat rate with a trend shift ([FEDS 2016-033](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econresdata/feds/2016/files/2016033pap.pdf)).

**Third, Bank Rate minus four-quarter backward core inflation mismeasures expected real rates around inflation surges.** HLW does use a four-quarter moving average of past inflation as its expectations proxy ([SR1063](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1063.pdf)). In the UK in 2021–23, however, that proxy made the measured real rate deeply negative while output did not boom. The filter can reconcile that only by lowering r*. This is my inference about the mechanism, and it should be tested by re-running with a forward-looking deflator. **Fourth, fixing a_r at −0.10 hides the identification problem instead of solving it.** HLW's estimated IS slopes are much flatter: −0.067 for the US, −0.065 for Canada and −0.037 for the euro area on pre-COVID samples ([SR1063](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1063.pdf)). The NY Fed's UK diagnosis ("weakened relationship") is precisely a slope heading towards the code's −0.0025 bound. Fiorentini et al. show that r* "cannot [be estimated] accurately when either the IS curve or the Phillips curve is flat" ([SSRN 3214487](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3214487)). Imposing a steep slope manufactures precision that the data do not contain.

The reported ±1.1 band is itself a warning. HLW's own average r* standard errors are **1.24 pp (US), 1.63 pp (Canada) and 3.48 pp (euro area)**, and they widen to 1.66, 1.84 and 4.82 pp at the sample end ([SR1063](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1063.pdf)). A UK estimate of −0.8 ± 1.1 is statistically compatible with the ~0.75–1.25% published range. The dashboard's failure is to let an uncertain point estimate drive a binary verdict.

## Four fixes turn HLW into a defensible growth-anchored estimator

The repair sequence is clear from the literature. **Fix 1: replace the MUE with Buncic's corrected Stage 2.** The Stage-2 IS equation should be a_y(L)ỹ_t = a_r(L)[r_t − 4g_t] + ε, with no constant (adding it raises the UK log-likelihood by only 0.0146). Run the break tests the Stock–Watson way, on an AR(4)-filtered series regressed only on a constant and a break dummy, with 15%/85% trimming rather than HLW's τ0 = 4 ([Buncic](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16452)). HLW 2023 has already adopted part of this, including the corrected y* timing (g_{t−1}, not g_{t−2}) ([SR1063](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1063.pdf)). Expect λz ≈ 0. The model then becomes, in effect, "r* = c × annualised trend growth", which is honest about what the UK data can identify. Publish the HLW-λz and ML/Bayesian-σz variants as sensitivity lines. Kiley shows that the r* variance posterior "moves with the prior" one-for-one (Δposterior/Δprior = 1.00), so the amount of time variation is a modelling choice that should be disclosed, not estimated away ([Kiley, IJCB 2020](https://www.ijcb.org/sites/default/files/journal/v16n3/ijcb-v16n3-what-can-data-tell-us-about-equilibrium-real-interest-rate.pdf)).

**Fix 2: adopt the HLW-2023 COVID treatment instead of dropping quarters.** HLW scale the measurement-error variances by κ for 2020Q2–Q4, 2021 and 2022 separately. They also add a persistent supply shock to potential output using the quarterly Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker (OxCGRT) Stringency Index, tapered linearly to zero over 2023Q1–2024Q4. Without these adjustments, 2020Q2 IS residuals were 9–17× the outlier threshold ([SR1063](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1063.pdf)). The UK stringency index is free, so the treatment is fully replicable. Dropping only the 2020 quarters leaves the 2021–22 volatility untreated. **Fix 3: make the real rate expectations-based and ELB-aware.** Deflate by a forward-looking one-year inflation expectation. Candidates are an AR-model forecast (Hamilton et al. use a rolling AR(4) on 40 quarters and find alternative expectation measures correlate at 0.98) or the BoE/Ipsos survey ([Hamilton et al.](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/WP16-Hamilton-et-al-equilibrium-real-funds-rate-1.pdf)). Over 2009–21, splice in a shadow rate. Wu–Xia UK runs monthly from January 1990 to February 2022 and its authors recommend splicing it with the policy rate ([Wu shadow rates](https://sites.google.com/view/jingcynthiawu/shadow-rates)). Krippner's UK shadow short rate runs from 1995 ([LJKmfa](https://www.ljkmfa.com/)). Shadow rates are model-dependent ([JMCB 2020 note of caution](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jmoncb/v52y2020i4p951-962.html)), so show one alternative that uses a 2–5-year gilt yield instead.

**Fix 4: estimate a_r and c, and publish diagnostics.** Keep HLW's bounds (b_y ≥ 0.025, a_r ≤ −0.0025; confirmed only via a code port, so [verify in the replication zip](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/economists/williams/data/HLW_2023_Replication_Code.zip)). Report whether a_r hits the bound, the MUE λ statistics and confidence intervals, filtered against smoothed r*, and recursive vintages. If a_r sits at the bound, the dashboard should say so and down-weight the model automatically. A cheap strengthening is Fiorentini et al.'s local-level equation for the observed real rate, which restores identification when the curves are flat ([CEPR DP13042](https://cepr.org/publications/dp13042)). If the policy-horizon concept matters, the Lewis–Vazquez-Grande variant that makes z an AR(1) with transitory shocks is the principled way to let r* move cyclically ([JAE 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jae.2671)). Even fully repaired, HLW should carry limited weight. Beyer and Wieland conclude that such methods "deliver imprecise and unstable results" ([JIMF 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0261560618303851)), and the ECB reports that HLW revisions have reached 1 pp between quarters ([ECB EB 1/2025](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/economic-bulletin/focus/2025/html/ecb.ebbox202501_08~3be5a005f9.en.html)).

## Trend-cycle models supply the ELB-robust core the BoE already uses

The strongest complement to HLW is the Del Negro–Giannone–Giannoni–Tambalotti (DGGT) "VAR with common trends", and a DGGT-based semi-structural model is one of the two BoE staff models cited in the February 2025 MPR ([BoE MPR Feb 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2025.pdf)). Observables split into random-walk trends and a stationary VAR cycle. In the 2017 US version, the short rate is r̄ + π̄ + cycle, inflation and survey expectations share π̄, and the long yield adds a trend term premium ([DGGT BPEA 2017](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/delnegrotextsp17bpea.pdf)). Its handling of the ELB is simple: the short rate after 2008Q3 is **treated as missing**, and long yields and surveys carry the information. Using the zero-bound data anyway gives "essentially the same" answers ([BPEA](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/delnegrotextsp17bpea.pdf)). Identification comes from tight priors. The trend-innovation variance mode is **1/400 per quarter for r̄** (about 1 pp of change per century) with κ_e = 100, and 1/200 for inflation. Initial trends are π̄ = 2, r̄ = 0.5 and term premium = 1, and the cycle VAR gets a Minnesota prior with tightness 0.2 and own-lag mean zero. Results barely change for trend horizons from a century down to a decade ([NBER w25039](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25039/w25039.pdf)). The global version already contains a UK trend built from annual Jordà–Schularick–Taylor data for 1870–2016. It finds country-specific trends "vanishing since the late 1970s", so the UK is now pinned largely by the world trend. BSD-licensed Matlab code exists ([rstarGlobal](https://github.com/FRBNY-TimeSeriesAnalysis/rstarGlobal)), and a numpy port with a Durbin–Koopman simulation smoother runs to a few hundred lines.

Johannsen–Mertens (JM) is the most principled ELB treatment. Observed rate = max(shadow rate, ELB). The trend real rate has constant-variance random-walk shocks, the gaps follow a VAR(2) with stochastic volatility, and draws are rejected until the shadow rate lies below the ELB in ELB quarters. Its US r̄ stays **between 1.7% and 2.2%**, far steadier than HLW ([FEDS 2016-033](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econresdata/feds/2016/files/2016033pap.pdf); [BIS WP 715](https://www.bis.org/publ/work715.pdf)). The code is Fortran/Matlab ([GitHub](https://github.com/elmarmertens/JohannsenMertensJMCBtimeseriesELB)), and with about 50 UK quarters at or near the ELB, rejection sampling would be slow. JM is therefore a phase-2 upgrade to the DGGT model, adding a censored short rate with a time-varying UK ELB (0.5/0.25/0.1%), rather than a first build. Davis et al.'s two-trend macro-finance model is the other BoE staff model. It links the average gilt yield to π* and r* through a no-arbitrage block and underlies Taylor's UK estimates, which carry 95% bands of about 50–150 bp ([Davis et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022199624000436); [Taylor, Jul 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2025/july/the-end-of-the-road-speech-by-alan-taylor.pdf)). A dashboard-sized DGGT model that includes a gilt-yield observable captures most of its information.

Moving averages remain useful as transparent benchmarks, provided they are labelled as benchmarks. Hamilton, Harris, Hatzius and West treat 40-quarter backward averages of the ex-ante real rate as "at best a noisy indicator". Their more robust "world long-run rate" is the cross-country median of AR(1) long-run means estimated on 30-year rolling windows ([Hamilton et al.](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/WP16-Hamilton-et-al-equilibrium-real-funds-rate-1.pdf)). DGGT show formally that decadal moving averages behave like a trend model with an extreme one-year horizon prior. They "conflate trends with cyclical variations", and the implied 50% bands are about 10 pp wide ([NBER w25039](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25039/w25039.pdf)). A UK 10-year MA spans the whole ELB decade, so it will read very low. It should appear on the chart but carry no weight. Unrestricted time-varying-parameter VARs are a poor fit for UK 2020–23 data: Lubik and Matthes had to restrict parameter drift in 2023 after "excessive specification flexibility" produced volatile estimates ([Richmond Fed EB 23-32](https://www.richmondfed.org/publications/research/economic_brief/2023/eb_23-32)).

## Markets and surveys bracket r* from above, with term premia in between

Market measures are free, daily and easy to compute, but the raw numbers are not r*. From the BoE's free GLC real curve, the UK 5y5y real forward (RPI-linked) has moved from **−1.52% (2018 average)** to **2.71% on 28 September 2026**, with the nominal 5y5y at 5.85% and implied RPI inflation 5y5y at 3.14% (own calculation from [BoE yield curves](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/statistics/yield-curves)). The Bank's own market-based measure (10-year forward real rates from nominal yields less survey inflation expectations) rose only by "over 90 basis points since 2018". The Bank warns that it "may overstate the true increase" because long yields are oversensitive to short yields and "estimates of term premia are uncertain" ([BoE MPR Feb 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2025.pdf)). The gap between a +4.2 pp raw ILG move and a +0.9 pp adjusted move reflects term premia, ILG-specific liability-driven investment (LDI) demand and the RPI basis. On the LDI point, index-linked gilts bore disproportionate selling in the 2022 crisis and the Bank bought £7.2bn of them ([BoE QB 2023](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/2023/2023/financial-stability-buy-sell-tools-a-gilt-market-case-study)). On the RPI basis, RPI aligns with CPIH from 2030, so today's 5y5y window lies post-reform, but the 2018 window did not ([MPR Feb 2025, Chart 2.28 note](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2025.pdf)). The distance between the ILG forward (≈2.7%) and survey-implied real neutral (≈1.25%) gives a rough upper-bound gauge of roughly 1.5 pp of term and ILG premia today. That is an inference with no published UK estimate to validate it.

Best practice does not discard market data. It either models the term premium or shows the raw measure beside the other classes with caveats. Christensen and Rudebusch strip real term and liquidity premia with an arbitrage-free Nelson–Siegel (AFNS) model on individual TIPS, with an average liquidity premium of 34 bp. A rival six-factor model, however, gives negative r* for almost the whole sample, which shows how model-dependent term-premium adjustments are ([FRBSF WP 2017-07](https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/wp2017-07.pdf)). Bauer and Rudebusch show that fixed-endpoint models mis-measure term premia when r* drifts ([AER 2020](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20171822)). The Bank's August 2025 and February 2026 MPRs use several UK term-premium models (Malik–Meldrum, Vlieghe, Andreasen–Meldrum, Meldrum–Roberts-Sklar), deflated by Consensus expectations. None of these is published as a free series ([MPR Aug 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/august/monetary-policy-report-august-2025.pdf); [MPR Feb 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2026/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2026.pdf)). Malik and Meldrum validate ACM-type regression term-structure models on UK data ([BoE SWP 518](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/working-paper/2014/evaluating-the-robustness-of-uk-term-structure-decompositions-using-linear-regression-methods)), so an in-house ACM on the free GLC nominal curve is the realistic phase-2 route. Taylor finds that model-based neutral-rate forecasts beat market forwards at predicting short rates, and that "the market was wrong for much of the 2010s" ([Taylor, ECB Forum 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/speech/2025/july/alan-taylor-panellist-at-ecb-forum-on-central-banking-2025)). That is another reason to keep raw forwards out of the headline.

The MaPS neutral rate is the most useful free UK survey series. Respondents give the Bank Rate "at which monetary policy is neither expansionary nor contractionary". The survey is published eight times a year since February 2022, with an XLSX file per round ([BoE, Jan 2022](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/news/2022/january/bank-of-england-to-publish-results-of-market-participants-survey)). The median was 3.00% in February 2023 and February 2024 and 3.50% in March 2025 ([Mar 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/markets/market-intelligence/survey-results/2025/market-participants-survey-results-march-2025)). It fell back to 3.00% in October 2025 ([Nov 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/markets/market-intelligence/survey-results/2025/market-participants-survey-results-november-2025)) and stood at **3.25% (IQR 3.00–3.50%) in September 2026**, with 92 respondents ([Sep 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/markets/market-intelligence/survey-results/2026/market-participants-survey-results-september-2026)). The Bank cautions that the question "does not clearly distinguish between the long-term equilibrium real rate and shorter-term concepts affected by cyclical factors" ([MPR Feb 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2025.pdf)). The 3.5→3.0→3.25 swings track the rate cycle, so the series belongs on the policy-horizon side of the dashboard. Consensus Economics' 6–10-year-ahead series, which rose by less than 25 bp since 2018, is licensed and should appear only as quoted context.

## Structural and global evidence pins UK r* near 0.3–1% real

Structural models explain where r* comes from better than they nowcast its level. Rachel and Smith attribute about 400 bp of a roughly 450 bp global decline mainly to saving and investment shifts: demographics, inequality and a cheaper relative price of capital ([BoE SWP 571](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/working-paper/2015/secular-drivers-of-the-global-real-interest-rate.pdf)). Cesa-Bianchi, Harrison and Sajedi's overlapping-generations (OLG) model puts global R* at about −0.75% by 2020, driven chiefly by slower productivity and longer lives ([BoE SWP 990](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/working-paper/2022/decomposing-the-drivers-of-global-r-star)). The IMF's 2023 OLG work projects advanced-economy natural rates "likely to stay close to pre-pandemic levels". Plausible shocks are tens of basis points, not percentage points: +5–10 bp for 25 pp more public debt, and about +70 bp if convenience yields return to pre-2000 levels ([IMF WEO Apr 2023 Ch. 2](https://www.imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/WEO/2023/April/English/ch2.ashx)). The latest NY Fed trend-VAR across 18 advanced economies finds that **global r* rose about 0.8 pp over 2019–24, to 0.31%**, with a 68% band of roughly −0.5% to above 1%, and attributes about a third of the move to the convenience yield ([Liberty Street, Feb 2026](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/02/the-post-pandemic-global-r/)).

For the UK as a small open economy, the Bank's doctrine is that R* "will ultimately be determined by global forces" ([MPR Feb 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2025.pdf)). Empirically there is a persistent local wedge. Morley and Wong find global shocks explain only "a quarter to a third" of the UK's r* decline, with more estimation uncertainty than for Canada or Sweden ([RBA workshop 2025](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/workshops/research/2025/pdf/rba-workshop-2025-morley-wong.pdf)). Taylor places UK r* above Japan and the euro area ([Taylor, Jul 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2025/july/the-end-of-the-road-speech-by-alan-taylor.pdf)). Comparing Taylor's 0.75% with the NY Fed global 0.31% implies a UK premium of about +0.4 pp. That is my inference across models, not a published figure. The Bank of Canada offers a replicable template: take the US as a proxy for global and adjust for domestic factors ([BoC SAN 2025-16](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2025/06/staff-analytical-note-2025-16/)). For the dashboard, structural work belongs in a "drivers" panel and a global-anchor line, not in the headline average.

## Central banks publish ranges by method class, never one model

No major central bank publishes a single official r* number. The Bank of England shows ranges by class: staff macro models (+25–75 bp since 2018), market measures (>+90 bp) and surveys (MaPS +150 bp, Consensus <+25 bp). It concludes that R* "is likely to have increased modestly" and "is not used by the MPC as a direct guide to setting policy" ([MPR Feb 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2025.pdf)). Its baseline is the August 2018 view of **2–3% nominal**. Its August 2025 and February 2026 MPRs present policy-horizon real-rate gaps by class and stress that restrictiveness is "highly dependent on the level of r*", recommending **nominal gaps as a cross-check**. In February 2026, with Bank Rate at 3.75%, the models showed "a small positive real interest rate gap, ranging from around 0–1 percentage point", and the ranges were wide enough that Bank Rate "may be around neutral" ([MPR Feb 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2026/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2026.pdf)).

Other banks differ mainly in how they aggregate. The ECB reports a min–max across four families, **−0.5% to +0.5% real** for Q4 2024 (up to +1% including HLW), and warns that the range covers model uncertainty only ([ECB EB 1/2025](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/economic-bulletin/focus/2025/html/ecb.ebbox202501_08~3be5a005f9.en.html)). The Bank of Canada builds its range from counterfactuals across three models, "rather than a simple average", rounded to 25 bp. It gives **2.25–3.25% nominal** for Canada and 2.50–3.50% for the US in 2026 ([BoC SAP 2026-21](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2026/05/staff-analytical-paper-2026-21/)). Norges Bank publishes a model-portfolio average of **0.4% real** ([Staff Memo 8/2025](https://www.norges-bank.no/en/news-events/publications/Staff-Memo/2025/staff-memo-8-2025/)). The St Louis Fed's "alternative r*" is the geometric mean of six measures, **1.43% at Q4 2025**, with HLW lowest (~1.0%) and 10y10y TIPS highest (>3%) ([St Louis Fed, May 2026](https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2026/may/comparing-fomc-estimate-r-star-alternative-estimates)). The RBA averages models but warns that "the average is sensitive to the choice of models included" ([RBA minutes Jul 2025](https://www.rba.gov.au/monetary-policy/rba-board-minutes/2025/2025-07-08.html)). None uses formal Bayesian model averaging in public. A simple, disclosed rule is therefore the accepted standard, and the dashboard's credibility depends more on showing the class structure and bands than on clever weights.

The rationale for the weights is as follows. The DGGT model is the anchor because it is ELB-robust, uses the gilt curve and surveys, and mirrors a BoE staff model. HLW keeps a minority weight because even repaired it is fragile for the UK. MaPS carries the survey lens the Bank itself uses. The raw ILG forward is excluded until premia are modelled, which matches the Bank's own caveat. A reasonable alternative to fixed weights is the **median of the core estimators**, which is robust to one model misbehaving. Whichever rule is chosen, disclose it, as the RBA warning implies.

### Real vs nominal, long-run vs policy-horizon

Present two panels. The **long-run R\*** panel shows DGGT r̄, the market-implied forward (labelled "includes term premium"), the global anchor and the structural drivers ledger. The **policy-horizon neutral** panel shows repaired HLW (filtered), MaPS, and the combined range used for the stance. Show every number in both real and nominal (+2%) form. For the stance, compute the ex-ante real policy rate as Bank Rate minus **one-year-ahead expected inflation**, not backward core inflation. Compare it to the real range and run the BoE's recommended nominal cross-check (Bank Rate against the nominal neutral range). Replace the binary verdict with graded bands. If the real rate is inside the range, the verdict is "around neutral". Within 0.5 pp above the top of the range it is "modestly restrictive", and more than 0.5 pp above it is "restrictive"; the accommodative side mirrors this. "Room to ease" should require a restrictive reading on both the real and nominal gaps. The acceptance tests follow from these benchmarks. The combined 2025Q1 headline should sit within 0.25–1.25% real. The change since 2018 should be positive and of the order of 25–100 bp. Repaired HLW at 2019Q4 should land near Buncic's 1.35–1.80%. Every estimator should sit below the raw ILG forward and, typically, above the NY Fed global line. Failing any of these should trigger investigation before publication, not a headline.

## Conclusion

The dashboard's "room to ease" verdict comes from applying a model the NY Fed abandoned for the UK, with parameter choices (λz = 0.03, a zero-bound real rate, a backward inflation deflator) that each push r* down. It does not come from any evidence that UK policy is unusually tight. The deeper lesson from Buncic and Kiley is that UK macro data carry very little information about the *level* of r*. Once the MUE is corrected, HLW becomes a trend-growth estimate, and any time variation beyond that is a prior. Credible UK r* estimates therefore draw their level from gilt yields, surveys and global trends, and use the macro filters mainly for direction and diagnostics.

That changes what the dashboard should communicate. The informative quantity is not "r* = x%" but "Bank Rate sits roughly 0.5–1 pp above a 2.5–3.25% nominal neutral zone that most methods agree on, and the methods disagree mainly about premia and horizon". Building the suite in the order above (repaired HLW, DGGT trend-cycle, MaPS, then a term-premium-adjusted market measure) gets the dashboard to Bank-of-England-style triangulation. Most of the gain comes from the second and third steps. The main open risk is the UK term premium: no free series exists, so until an in-house ACM model is validated, the market lens should inform the upper bound and stay out of the headline.
