# Pass-through of energy, commodity, import-price and exchange-rate shocks to UK inflation, wages and expectations (incl. state dependence)

*Research notes compiled 29 Sept 2026. The literature covers up to Sept 2026, including the 2026 Middle East/Strait of Hormuz energy shock, which gives a live out-of-sample test of the 2022 lessons. Figures marked "(search summary)" came from search-engine summaries of the primary source and were not checked against the full text.*

## 1. Exchange-rate pass-through (ERPT) to UK import prices and CPI, including the 2008 and 2016 depreciations

### Takeaway
The long-run UK rule of thumb is about 60% pass-through to import prices and about 20% to CPI, so a 10% sterling depreciation raises CPI by about 2% over 6–8 quarters or longer. Forbes, Hjortsoe & Nenova show this varies a lot with the shock behind the move:
- Pass-through is high after monetary and global shocks, and low or even negative after domestic demand shocks.
- This explains why pass-through was high in 2007–09 (about 90% to import prices) and low after the 2013–15 appreciation.
- Their model predicted lower pass-through after the 2016 referendum depreciation (about 40–45% to import prices).
- Micro evidence (Breinlich et al.) nonetheless finds near-complete pass-through of import costs to the prices of affected products after 2016. The aggregate ERPT was about 0.29, and consumer prices were about 2.9% higher.

### Cited Findings
- **Rule of thumb:** long-run ERPT to UK import prices is "about 60%, and to consumer prices is about 20%", i.e. "a 10% appreciation would be expected to reduce import prices by 6% and consumer prices by about 2% in the long run". The 20% equals 60% × a 30% share of imported inputs in CPI. — [Forbes, Hjortsoe & Nenova (2018), NBER WP 24773, p.~6](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24773/w24773.pdf)
- **BoE assumption, Nov 2015:** the Bank lowered its assumed ERPT to import prices "from about 90% (as observed after sterling's depreciation in 2007-9) to a base case of 60%" (Inflation Report box "The effect of imported price pressures on UK consumer prices", Nov 2015). — [Forbes et al. 2018, fn. 27](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24773/w24773.pdf)
- **Shock-dependent ERPT to import prices** (1% appreciation, cumulative):
  - Domestic demand shock: less than 40% after 5 quarters.
  - Monetary policy shock: almost 70% within 2 quarters and 85% by quarter 6.
  - Domestic supply shock: 67% after 5 quarters.
  - Exogenous exchange-rate shock: 50% after 5 quarters.
  - The reduced-form "rule of thumb" is close to the average of the four domestic shocks.
  - [Forbes et al. 2018, pp.15–16](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24773/w24773.pdf)
- **Timing:** exchange-rate moves reach import prices fully in about 4 quarters and consumer prices in "around six to eight quarters".
  - For five of six shocks, consumer prices move by about 20% of the initial exchange-rate move after 8 quarters.
  - Appreciations driven by domestic demand shocks *raise* CPI (a positive "pass-through" coefficient).
  - [Forbes et al. 2018, pp.22–23](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24773/w24773.pdf)
- **2007–09 vs 2013–15:**
  - The mix of shocks behind the 2007–09 depreciation raised pass-through to import prices by almost one-third, and roughly doubled it to consumer prices, relative to 1996–97.
  - Using the 2007–08 decomposition, a 10% appreciation would have lowered CPI by 1.7–1.8%. The actual 2013–15 shock mix implied only 0.1–1%.
  - For import prices, the 2007–09 mix implies that a 10% move changes import prices by about 9%, versus about 7% on average (4–7% on the 2013–15 mix).
  - [Forbes et al. 2018, pp.2, 21–24](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24773/w24773.pdf)
- **2016 referendum, real-time forecast:**
  - Sterling fell almost 13% (vs 2015Q4) within two weeks of the vote.
  - The model attributed 46% of the 2016 depreciation to domestic demand shocks, 36% to exchange-rate shocks and 14% to domestic supply shocks.
  - It predicted ERPT to import prices of 40% after 4 quarters and about 45% in the long run. That compares with >90% after the financial crisis and about 80% for the full sample.
  - The authors say later price data supported this forecast.
  - [Forbes et al. 2018, pp.29–31](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24773/w24773.pdf); published version: [JIE 114 (2018) 255–275](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022199618302009); BoE version: [External MPC Unit DP (2015)](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/external-mpc-discussion-paper/2015/the-shocks-matter-improving-our-estimates-of-exchange.pdf)
- **BoE Nov 2016 Inflation Report:**
  - CPI rises by about 30% of any rise in import prices.
  - Historically the peak effect on inflation came after about a year, and inflation kept being pushed up for about three more years.
  - The MPC judged that pass-through from non-energy import prices would be faster than in the past this time.
  - (search summary) — [BoE Inflation Report Nov 2016](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/inflation-report/2016/november-2016.pdf)
- **Micro evidence on 2016 (Breinlich, Leromain, Novy & Sampson):**
  - Results are "consistent with complete pass-through of the cost of imports to consumer prices".
  - Implied aggregate ERPT is 0.29.
  - The Brexit depreciation raised consumer prices by 2.9%, costing the average household about £870 a year.
  - (search summary of abstract) — [Breinlich et al., International Economic Review 63(1) 2022, 63–93](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/iecrev/v63y2022i1p63-93.html); [CEP DP](https://cep.lse.ac.uk/_NEW/PUBLICATIONS/abstract.asp?index=9442)
- **ONS (2019) on 2015–16:**
  - Import price inflation rose from −7.2% (Aug 2015) to 13.6% (Jan 2017), a 20.8pp swing.
  - CPI inflation went from about zero in 2015 to a 3.1% peak in Nov 2017.
  - Goods inflation went from −2% to over 3%.
  - Half of the rise in CPI inflation came from items with more than 25% import intensity.
  - The impact on output PPI and CPI was smaller and more lagged than on import prices and input PPI.
  - Direct imports are about 13% and indirect imports about 8% of household final consumption.
  - [ONS Economic Review, July 2019](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/uksectoraccounts/compendium/economicreview/july2019/exchangeratepassthroughandtransmissiontoconsumerpricesfollowingthe2015to2016depreciationofsterling/pdf)

### Inferences
- Combining the rule of thumb with the ONS timing gives a reasonable mapping: a 10% sterling fall → about +6% import prices within about 1 year → about +2% on the CPI level over 2–3 years. That is roughly +0.7–1pp a year on inflation at the peak. The Nov 2017 peak of 3.1% after a roughly 20%-plus fall in 2015–16 is broadly consistent with this.
- The 2016 evidence is mixed:
  - The SVAR predicted *lower* macro pass-through (a demand-driven depreciation).
  - Product-level work finds full pass-through into imported-intensive items, yet an aggregate ERPT of only about 0.29, close to the 0.2–0.3 rule of thumb.
  - These are not strictly contradictory: macro vs micro, and import prices vs CPI.
- Pass-through tends to be higher when the move reflects monetary or global shocks. This matters for 2022-type episodes, where a global energy shock and sterling weakness coincided.

### Gaps
- I could not open the BoE Nov 2015 IR box or the BoE Inflation Report 2017 boxes to confirm the exact ERPT profile the Bank used after 2016 (e.g. "pass-through of around X% to CPI over 3 years").
- The Forbes (2016) speech "Much ado about something important" was not reviewed.
- No post-2020 UK-specific ERPT estimate was found, e.g. for the Sept 2022 "mini-budget" sterling fall.

## 2. Energy price pass-through: direct (Ofgem cap) vs indirect effects on core/services; size and speed

### Takeaway
**Direct effects** are large and fast. They are lagged and smoothed by the Ofgem price cap for utilities and near-immediate for fuel. Household energy was the main single driver of the 11.1% peak in Oct 2022, and the Energy Price Guarantee (EPG) held CPI about 2.7pp lower than it would otherwise have been.

**Indirect (supply-chain) effects** were about 1pp of CPI at their 2022Q4 peak (BoE input-output estimate). They were concentrated in food (about 3pp of food inflation) and in some services (transport, restaurants). Pass-through is incomplete, especially in services, and slow:
- Long-run coefficient of about 0.8 for manufacturing and 0.4 for services.
- About 8 quarters (manufacturing) and 15 quarters (services) to reach 80% of pass-through.

Model-based UK estimates of a *typical* (pre-pandemic-sized) energy shock are small: about 0.2pp on annual CPI. In 2021 alone, energy contributed about 1.7pp to annualised quarterly inflation. In 2026 the MPC expected indirect effects of about 0.3pp by Q3 but saw less than that.

### Cited Findings
- **Oct 2022 CPI (ONS):**
  - CPI was 11.1% (41-year high) and CPIH 9.6%. Core CPI stayed at 6.5%.
  - Housing & household services (electricity, gas) added about 0.93pp to the *change* in the rate between Sept and Oct.
  - Without the EPG, electricity, gas and other fuel prices would have risen about 75% rather than about 25%, taking CPI to about **13.8%** instead of 11.1%.
  - [ONS CPI bulletin, Oct 2022](https://ons.gov.uk/economy/inflationandpriceindices/bulletins/consumerpriceinflation/october2022)
- **Ofgem cap and EPG:** Ofgem planned an 80% rise in the cap on 1 Oct 2022; the EPG limited it to 27%. (search summary) — [House of Commons Library, Domestic energy prices](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-9491/)
- **Energy's share of the 11.1% peak:** energy contributed "approximately 4 percentage points" (search summary; source not verified — treat as indicative). — [House of Commons Library CBP-9714](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-9714/)
- **Indirect supply-chain effects (Mrabet & Page, BoE):**
  - Energy and food cost pass-through through the supply chain "boosted CPI inflation by around 1 percentage point at peak (2022 Q4)".
  - By component: about 3pp to food and non-alcoholic beverages inflation (peak 2022Q3), about 1pp each to transport services and restaurants & hotels.
  - Long-run pass-through coefficients: 0.8 for manufacturing, 0.4 for services.
  - Time to pass 80% of a shock: 8 quarters (manufacturing), 15 quarters (services).
  - Cost decreases take about 2 quarters longer to pass through than increases (asymmetry).
  - Method: error-correction models for about 70 sectors, 1997 onwards, using Supply-Use tables and PPI/SPPI.
  - [Bank Underground, 24 Aug 2023](https://bankunderground.co.uk/2023/08/24/how-do-firms-pass-energy-and-food-costs-through-the-supply-chain/)
- **Bernanke–Blanchard model for the UK (Haskel, Martin & Brandt 2025, 1990–2024):**
  - A typical pre-pandemic relative-energy-price shock adds "a little under 0.2pp" to annual CPI, roughly flat over 4 quarters, with little effect on q/q inflation after the first quarter. Energy shocks are *less persistent* than food shocks.
  - A typical food shock peaks at +0.3pp after 4 quarters and still adds about 0.02pp after 4 years.
  - 2022 shocks were far larger: the maximum quarterly relative-energy shock in 2022Q2 was about a 25% q/q rise relative to wages. The 2020Q1–2023Q1 standard deviation of relative energy prices was 4.3× its pre-pandemic level.
  - In 2021Q1–Q4, annualised q/q CPI averaged 4.8%. Energy contributed 1.7pp and supply chains 0.8pp.
  - Regulated prices delayed pass-through: wholesale rises in 2021 hit CPI mainly via the Oct 2021 and Apr 2022 cap changes.
  - [ESCoE DP 2025-12 (PDF)](https://escoe-website.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/24140421/ESCoE-DP-2025-12-2.pdf); [landing page](https://www.escoe.ac.uk/publications/what-explains-recent-uk-inflation-an-application-of-the-bernanke-blanchard-model/)
- **Oil vs gas:** the Bank's energy BVAR (Copeland et al., 2025) finds oil supply shocks have "more immediate but short-lived effects on UK inflation, while gas supply shocks tend to have broader and more persistent effects". Gas is the marginal price-setter for UK electricity. — [Greene speech, 2 June 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **Size non-linearity:** large supply shocks create "supply-chain pricing cascades" (Ghassibe & Nakov 2025, euro area). "Bank analysis using UK data also finds non-linearities stemming from the size of the energy shock." — [Greene 2026, fn. 1](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **Energy CPI inflation peaks:** 18% in 2011 vs 59% in 2022. — [Greene 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **2011 vs 2022 output-price decomposition (Bank staff):**
  - In both episodes firms passed on energy costs and indirect costs.
  - In 2022 unit labour costs were a much bigger contributor (second-round), and margins were compressed afterwards.
  - In 2011 margin *rebuilding* was a bigger driver.
  - Source: industry dataset introduced in Nov 2025 MPR Box A — [Greene 2026, Fig. 14](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **2026 shock, direct effects:**
  - The April 2026 CPI recorded the largest monthly rise in fuel prices since at least 1988: forecourt prices up about 15% m/m and 23% y/y. [Greene 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
  - Petrol rose from about 132p to 172p a litre between Feb and Sept 2026.
  - The Ofgem cap rises nearly 4% in Oct 2026 (to £1,723 a year) and could exceed £2,000 in 2027Q1.
  - [Lombardelli speech, 24 Sept 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/speech/2026/september/clare-lombardelli-speech-at-the-sixth-biennial-conference-poland)
- **2026 shock, indirect effects:**
  - The MPC projected indirect effects of about 0.3pp on August 2026 CPI, but there was "little evidence that firms are passing higher energy costs through to the prices of other goods". Food inflation came in below projection.
  - CPI is projected to rise from 3.1% to about 3.7% in 2026Q4 and 4.2% in 2027Q1 (about 4.5% without mitigating measures).
  - [Lombardelli, 24 Sept 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/speech/2026/september/clare-lombardelli-speech-at-the-sixth-biennial-conference-poland)
  - The Bank expected indirect effects to raise CPI by about ⅓pp in Q3, fastest in food, with core goods and services also affected; "indirect energy pass-through has been weaker than expected so far". (search summary) — [BoE MPR July 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/monetary-policy-report/2026/july-2026)
- **April 2026 MPR scenarios:**
  - Scenario A: CPI a little over 3½% at end-2026, ending a little below 2%.
  - Scenario B (energy stays higher; modest second-round effects): a little over 3½%, ending close to 2%.
  - Scenario C (2022-like oil, persistent gas, larger second-round effects): peak over 6% in early 2027, ending around 2½%.
  - [BoE MPR April 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/monetary-policy-report/2026/april-2026)
  - Scenario B assumes oil at $108/bbl and gas at 114p/therm. Its second-round effects are calibrated on the Haskel et al. Bernanke–Blanchard UK model. Scenario C adds amplification via long-run expectations. [Greene 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **IMF 2026 Article IV:** pass-through to core "should be more limited, given the weak labor market, slow wage growth, and a negative output gap". It assumes gas prices +21.7% and oil +31.8% in 2026. — [IMF 2026 Article IV press release / staff report](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/cr/2026/english/1gbrea2026001.pdf)
- **Cross-country comparison (IMF, Alvarez & Kroen 2025, 30+ countries):**
  - In advanced economies, a 1pp rise in energy inflation passes through about 0.05–0.07pp into CPI inflation over six quarters.
  - Pass-through "appears to be relatively stable over time". Non-linearities in advanced economies "were already present pre-Covid and did not strengthen significantly after 2020".
  - Energy-dependent, highly connected and flexible-price sectors show much larger pass-through.
  - [IMF WP 2025/091, "The Energy Origins of the Global Inflation Surge"](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2025/english/wpiea2025091-print-pdf.pdf)

### Inferences
- A rough UK decomposition for 2022:
  - Direct household energy: roughly 4pp of the 11.1% peak (indicative).
  - Supply-chain indirect effects: about 1pp at the 2022Q4 peak.
  - Second-round effects (wages, expectations): see Section 3.
  - The EPG removed about 2.7pp at the peak.
- Most of the energy shock's effect on core and services arrives in years 1–3, not in the first months, and is only partial for services (a coefficient of about 0.4).
- Because of the Ofgem cap, gas/electricity shocks reach UK CPI with a lag of about 3–6 months and in steps (cap changes in Oct 2021, Apr 2022, Oct 2022). Oil reaches CPI within weeks.
- The 2026 episode so far shows smaller indirect pass-through than models predicted. That fits state dependence: slack, weaker demand and compressed margins.

### Gaps
- I did not locate a published BoE rule of thumb of the form "a 10% oil price rise adds X pp to headline and Y pp to core". Greene's threshold-VAR figure uses a 10% sterling oil news shock, but the impulse-response magnitudes are only in the chart.
- I did not retrieve specific BoE MPR boxes from 2022–23 quantifying the direct energy contribution (e.g. Nov 2022 MPR).
- I did not retrieve OBR EFO or OECD UK-specific pass-through numbers.

## 3. Second-round effects on wages, firm pricing and inflation expectations (DMP, Agents, wage–price models)

### Takeaway
In 2022–23 the UK saw material second-round effects, mainly through the wage channel. Bernanke–Blanchard UK estimates:
- A catch-up channel added about 1pp to wage growth (2022Q1–2023Q2) and about 0.4pp to inflation.
- Labour-market tightness added 0.5–1.8pp to inflation over 2022–23.
- Energy, food and supply-chain shocks together added about 1pp to wage growth through expectations and catch-up.
- Long-run expectations stayed broadly anchored in the model.

Firms' price expectations respond to headline CPI: +1pp CPI → +0.3pp expected own-price growth (DMP). Household expectations respond strongly to salient petrol prices when media coverage is high. In 2026, household 1-year expectations jumped from 3.3% to 5.4% within a month of the shock.

### Cited Findings
- **Wage equation (Haskel, Martin & Brandt 2025):**
  - Sum of coefficients: lagged wages 0.60 (US 0.46); expectations 0.40 (US 0.54). The UK has stickier wages.
  - The V/U coefficient sum is 2.10. A rise in V/U from 1.0 to 1.5 raises long-run wage growth by 2.6pp, so UK wages are more sensitive to tightness than US wages.
  - V/U tightness contributes 1.6pp to wage growth on average in 2022, 0.7pp in 2023 and 0.7pp in 2024Q1.
  - Energy, food and supply chains, through expectations and catch-up, add about 1pp to wage growth over 2022–23.
  - The catch-up channel adds about 1.0pp to annualised wage growth (2022Q1–2023Q2) and about 0.4pp to inflation (2022Q1–2023Q4).
  - Tightness adds 0.5–1.8pp to inflation in 2022–23, depending on the counterfactual.
  - In early 2024, tightness still contributed about 0.7pp to wage growth and 0.6pp to price inflation.
  - [ESCoE DP 2025-12](https://escoe-website.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/24140421/ESCoE-DP-2025-12-2.pdf)
- **Expectations equations (same paper):**
  - Short-run expectations: own-lag 0.85, long-run expectations 0.13, realised inflation 0.02 (US: 0.37 / 0.51 / 0.12).
  - Long-run expectations: own-lag 0.99, current inflation 0.01.
  - "Inflation expectations have been more well-anchored than predicted by the model."
  - [ESCoE DP 2025-12](https://escoe-website.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/24140421/ESCoE-DP-2025-12-2.pdf)
- **Firms' expectations (Yotzov et al. 2024, DMP, cited by Greene):**
  - +1pp headline inflation is associated with +0.3pp in firms' expected year-ahead own-price growth.
  - The response is asymmetric: stronger when inflation rises than when it falls.
  - A 1 s.d. rise in media coverage of inflation → +0.4pp in own-price expectations.
  - Firms update CPI perceptions the same day as data releases.
  - [Greene 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **DMP in the 2026 shock:**
  - Firms' year-ahead own-price growth expectations rose from 3.4% (start of 2026) to 4.4% (April 2026).
  - 64% of firms expect to raise prices in response to the energy shock; 68% expect margins to be squeezed.
  - Output-price PMI rose by less than input prices, indicating partial absorption.
  - [Greene 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **DMP in 2020–22 ("Firming up price inflation", Bunn, Anayi, Bloom, Mizen, Thwaites & Yotzov, NBER WP 30505 / BoE SWP 2022):** energy prices and labour and material shortages account for most of the rise in firms' price growth during the rebound. (search summary) — [NBER / BoE SWP](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/working-paper/2022/firming-up-price-inflation)
- **Household expectations and petrol prices (Bank staff, Citi/YouGov, 2017–Apr 2026):**
  - When media coverage of energy is in its top quartile, a 1pp change in inflation driven by petrol prices moves household expectations by nearly 3pp short-term and about 0.5pp longer-term.
  - Sensitivity to utilities is smaller but significant at the 5-year horizon.
  - Food prices are also salient (Anesti, Esady & Naylor 2025).
  - [Greene 2026, Fig. 2](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **Anchoring since the pandemic:** households' (IAS, 5y on 1y) and firms' (DMP, 3y on 1y) long-run expectations have become more sensitive to short-term inflation surprises, i.e. "less solidly anchored than previously". — [Greene 2026, Fig. 3](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **Household 1-year expectations (Citi/YouGov):**
  - Nearly 5% in early 2022, then rose sharply.
  - 2026: 3.3% in Feb, 5.4% in March, 4.7% in May.
  - [Greene 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **Real wages, 2011 vs 2022:** in 2022 workers were able to push back against real-wage erosion. The labour-leverage ratio (job-to-job moves over redundancies) was much higher in 2022. Unemployment was about 4% in 2022 vs about 8% in 2011. — [Greene 2026, Figs. 10–12](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **2026 wages:** "Private sector wage growth has continued to slow"; judging second-round effects is "too early to tell". — [Lombardelli, 24 Sept 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/speech/2026/september/clare-lombardelli-speech-at-the-sixth-biennial-conference-poland)

### Inferences
- In the UK, most of the 2022–23 second-round effect ran through wages driven by labour-market tightness and catch-up (backward-looking indexation), not through de-anchored long-run expectations. The wage persistence coefficient (0.6) makes UK effects slower to fade than US ones.
- Energy prices affect household expectations disproportionately because of salience (petrol and utility bills). UK energy shocks therefore feed expectations faster than their CPI weight would suggest.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Resolution Foundation or NIESR quantitative wage–price spiral estimates, or BoE Agents' pay-survey numbers for 2022–23 (e.g. the share of settlements citing the cost of living).
- I did not find a quantitative estimate of the share of the energy shock that reached services CPI specifically through wages.

## 4. State dependence: is pass-through larger when inflation is high, labour markets are tight or expectations are less anchored?

### Takeaway
Strong recent UK evidence (BoE, 2025–26) supports state dependence:
- **Inflation threshold:** shocks have bigger effects when inflation exceeds about 3–4%; the headline CPI threshold is estimated at about 3.1%, with wage growth at about 4.6%.
- **Labour-market interaction:** oil-shock responses are larger and more persistent in tight labour markets, especially above the inflation threshold.
- **Pricing behaviour:** the share of firms using state-dependent pricing rose from 44% (2019) to about 60% (2023), and these firms pass on cost shocks faster, more so for large shocks.

The BIS "two-regime" view and ECB work agree. One IMF cross-country study is the main dissent: it finds energy pass-through non-linearities that were already present pre-2020 and did not intensify.

### Cited Findings
- **Inflation thresholds (BoE Nov 2025 MPR, Box C):** "economic shocks tend to have a bigger impact on inflation when inflation is already above 3% to 4%." — [BoE MPR Nov 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/monetary-policy-report/2025/november-2025)
- **Threshold estimates (Gaffney & Potjagailo 2025; Gaffney et al. 2026; self-exciting threshold BVAR, 1989–2025):**
  - Household expectations become more sensitive to oil shocks above a headline CPI threshold of about **3.1%**.
  - Across 100 specifications, 70% find a threshold below 3.6%, and about half find one in 3–3.2%.
  - The wage-growth threshold is about **4.6%**. It was well below this in 2011 and above it in 2022.
  - Headline and food inflation were within or above their thresholds in both 2011 and 2022. Services was within its threshold range in 2011 and just below it in 2022, entering soon after.
  - [Greene 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **Labour-market interaction:**
  - Generalised impulse responses to a Känzig oil supply news shock that raises sterling oil prices 10% on impact show "inflation responds more forcefully and persistently in a tight labour market than a loose one", whether or not inflation is above 3.1%. "The difference becomes more extreme when inflation is high."
  - Household expectations rose after oil shocks in tight labour markets but stayed stable in loose ones.
  - [Greene 2026, Fig. 5](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **2011 vs 2022 as natural experiment:**
  - The Boosted Inflation Model trend component (Buckmann, Potjagailo & Schnattinger 2025) shows second-round effects were "larger and more persistent in 2022 than 2011".
  - Greene attributes the difference to shock size (energy CPI peaked at 18% in 2011 vs 59% in 2022, and gas dominated in 2022) and to slack (unemployment about 8% vs 4%, a negative vs positive output gap).
  - [Greene 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2026/here-we-go-again-assessing-the-inflation-risks-of-the-recent-energy-shock-speech-by-megan-greene.pdf)
- **State- vs time-dependent pricing (Bunn, Bloom, Menzies, Mizen, Thwaites & Yotzov, BoE SWP 1166, Jan 2026, DMP, 4,200+ firms):**
  - 54% of firms were state-dependent on average in 2023–25 (close to 60% in 2023, 50% in early 2025, 54% in Aug–Oct 2025), vs 44% in 2019.
  - State-dependent firms had a sharper rise in price growth in 2022–23 and a faster decline afterwards.
  - Their prices "respond more strongly to cost shocks", and the gap "is furthermore larger for bigger shocks". Evidence comes from a randomised survey experiment, forecast errors and local projections.
  - Goods firms and firms with high non-labour cost shares are more likely to be state-dependent.
  - [BoE SWP 1166](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/working-paper/2026/state-and-time-dependent-pricing.pdf)
- **Price-change frequency:**
  - Earlier UK micro evidence (Bunn & Ellis 2012) found that inflation variation is driven by the *frequency* of price increases rather than their size. (search summary)
  - Euro area CPI microdata: monthly frequency of price changes was 12% in 2022 vs an 8% average over 2010–19, falling quickly in 2023. (search summary)
  - [BoE SWP 1166](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/working-paper/2026/state-and-time-dependent-pricing.pdf)
- **BIS "two-regime view" (Borio, Lombardi, Yetman & Zakrajšek, BIS Papers 133, March 2023):**
  - Inflation is "self-stabilising in a low-inflation regime but loses that property as it shifts to the high-inflation one".
  - In the low regime, sectoral price changes co-move little and relative-price shocks have transient effects. Transitions to the high regime are self-reinforcing.
  - [BIS Papers 133](https://www.bis.org/publ/bppdf/bispap133.htm)
- **ECB comparison:**
  - "Pass-through of energy price increases to consumer inflation can be amplified when inflation is already high", as firms reprice more often. Oil shocks transmit faster than gas shocks.
  - Initial conditions: euro area headline was 5–6% in Feb 2022 vs slightly below 2% in early 2026. Vacancy rates were 2.2% in 2026Q1, well below the late-2021 highs.
  - [ECB Blog, Arce et al., 3 June 2026](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/blog/date/2026/html/ecb.blog20260603~3015f38292.en.html)
  - With data through 2023, core inflation's response to energy is "much stronger", especially in the euro area, suggesting a break in the core–energy relationship. (search summary) — [VoxEU, impact of energy shocks on core inflation in the US and euro area](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/impact-energy-shocks-core-inflation-us-and-euro-area); [VoxEU, nonlinear impact of retail energy supply shocks](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/nonlinear-impact-retail-energy-supply-shocks-inflation-and-economic-activity)
- **Contrary evidence (Alvarez & Kroen, IMF WP 2025/091):** non-linearities in energy→CPI pass-through in advanced economies "were already present pre-Covid and did not strengthen significantly after 2020". The size of pass-through conditional on the size of the shock "remains relatively predictable". — [IMF WP 2025/091](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2025/english/wpiea2025091-print-pdf.pdf)
- **Exchange-rate state dependence:** Forbes et al. find ERPT depends on the shock mix. ERPT is higher when monetary or global shocks drive sterling (2007–09) and low when domestic demand does (2013–15, 2016). Lewis (2016, cited there) finds higher ERPT to UK import prices for large moves than small ones. — [Forbes et al. 2018](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24773/w24773.pdf)

### Inferences
- The BoE's current working view has three parts:
  1. A regime threshold around 3–3.5% headline CPI and about 4.6% wage growth.
  2. Labour-market tightness as the key amplifier.
  3. A lasting post-pandemic shift toward more state-dependent pricing and less-anchored long-run expectations, meaning faster pass-through for future shocks.
- The 2026 episode is a partial test. Headline CPI was about 3.1–3.3% (at the threshold) and expectations jumped. But the labour market was loose and indirect pass-through came in *below* forecast by Sept 2026. This fits slack as a binding condition.
- The IMF result (stable non-linearity) and the BoE/ECB results (regime-dependence) can be reconciled: much of the 2022 amplification can be explained by shock *size* interacting with a pre-existing convexity, plus the labour-market state, rather than by a structural break.
