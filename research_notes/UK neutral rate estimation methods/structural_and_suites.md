# Structural (saving–investment, demographic, productivity, global) approaches to r* and how central banks combine multiple r* estimates — UK focus

Notes compiled 29 Sep 2026. All rates are **real** unless marked "nominal". "Neutral nominal" ≈ real r* + 2% inflation target (the convention used by BoE MPC members, BoC, ECB).

---

## 1. What does the structural / long-run-determinants literature say about UK and global r* levels and drivers?

### Takeaway
The structural literature agrees that global r* fell by roughly 3–4½ pp from the 1980s/1990s to below ~0–1% by the late 2010s, driven mainly by ageing/longevity, slower productivity growth, inequality and safe-asset demand, partly offset by rising public debt. Since 2019, estimates show a modest rebound: roughly +0.25 to +0.8 pp. The "higher r* after 2022" forces (fiscal, AI, green investment, financial fragmentation) are real but uncertain in size. Most structural modellers (the IMF WEO 2023, Obstfeld, Cesa-Bianchi et al., Taylor) expect advanced-economy r* to stay near pre-pandemic lows, around 0–1% real.

### Cited Findings

**Historical decline, magnitudes and drivers**
- Rachel & Smith (BoE Staff WP 571, 2015): long-term real rates worldwide fell ~450 bp over 30 years. They account for ~400 bp of this. Slowing global growth explains part of it, but "shifts in saving and investment preferences appear more important": demographics, higher inequality and (to a lesser extent) EM precautionary saving on the saving side; a falling relative price of capital, lower public investment and a wider spread between risk-free and actual rates on the investment side — [BoE WP 571](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/working-paper/2015/secular-drivers-of-the-global-real-interest-rate.pdf); published version "Are Low Real Interest Rates Here to Stay?" [IJCB 2017](https://www.ijcb.org/journal/ijcb17q3a1.pdf)
- Rachel & Summers (BPEA, Spring 2019): the neutral real rate in industrial economies fell ~3 pp over a generation. The "private-sector neutral real rate" (ex-fiscal support) may have fallen by as much as ~7 pp since the 1970s. Without offsetting fiscal policy (higher debt and social insurance), neutral rates would "in all likelihood be significantly negative", so mature economies are prone to secular stagnation — [Brookings BPEA](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/on-falling-neutral-real-rates-fiscal-policy-and-the-risk-of-secular-stagnation/); [PDF](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/RachelSummers_web.pdf)
- Cesa-Bianchi, Harrison & Sajedi (BoE Staff WP 990, 2022; "Global R*"): a structural OLG model with five drivers (productivity growth, population growth, longevity, government debt, relative price of capital), estimated on a 31-country panel for 1950–2020. Global R* rises until the mid-1970s, peaking at ~2.5%, then falls by more than 3 pp, driven mainly by slower productivity growth and higher longevity. By the end of the sample the model simulation is at ~−0.75%. Longevity "is not expected to unwind", and "without a reversal in these trends… long-run Global R* is likely to remain low" — [BoE WP 990](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/working-paper/2022/decomposing-the-drivers-of-global-r-star); [Bank Underground, Nov 2023](https://bankunderground.co.uk/2023/11/30/global-r/)
- Platzer & Peruffo (IMF WP 2022/030): a heterogeneous-agent OLG model of the US explains a 2.2 pp fall in r* from 1975 to 2015. The decomposition is productivity slowdown −1.0 pp, demographics −0.71 pp, income inequality −0.70 pp, and rising public debt +0.31 pp (offsetting) — [IMF WP 22/30](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WP/Issues/2022/02/11/Secular-Drivers-of-the-Natural-Rate-of-Interest-in-the-United-States-A-Quantitative-512755)
- IMF WEO April 2023, Ch. 2 ("The Natural Rate of Interest: Drivers and Implications for Policy"):
  - HLW estimates for six advanced economies show r* fell by "a little over 2 percentage points in most countries" between the late 1970s and late 2010s. Actual real rates fell ~5 pp over the same period.
  - The uncertainty is "very large": the US 90% confidence interval runs from zero to about 3% in the late 2010s.
  - A structural OLG model covering US, UK, France, Germany, Japan, China, India and Brazil (~70% of world GDP) projects that advanced-economy natural rates "are likely to stay close to pre-pandemic levels". Further ageing has only a "moderate" residual effect, and higher public debt acts as a counterweight.
  - Conclusion: "once the current inflationary episode has passed, interest rates are likely to revert toward pre-pandemic levels in advanced economies" — [IMF WEO Apr 2023 Ch. 2](https://www.imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/WEO/2023/April/English/ch2.ashx); [eLibrary](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9798400224119/CH002.xml)
- IMF WEO 2023 alternative scenarios (deviations from baseline by 2050). Individually they span "about 120 basis points centered on the baseline":
  - public debt +25% of GDP → at most +5 to +10 bp for most countries
  - erosion of the safe-asset convenience yield back to pre-2000 levels → ~+70 bp in advanced economies (up to ~+100 bp in the US if foreign portfolio inflows reversed)
  - labour share returning to mid-1970s levels → +6 to +19 bp
  - Paris-consistent energy transition → about −50 bp by 2050 on a hump-shaped path, or temporarily +30 bp if deficit-financed
  - deglobalisation / financial fragmentation → depends on external position (deficit countries up, surplus countries down)
  - Source: [IMF WEO Apr 2023 Ch. 2](https://www.imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/WEO/2023/April/English/ch2.ashx)
- ECB OP 217 (Brand, Bielecki & Penalver, 2018): "Using a wide range of models we document a protracted fall" in r*, driven by ageing, waning productivity growth, rising mark-ups and a surge in risk aversion after the GFC. Estimates are "highly uncertain and model dependent", and most were negative after the financial crisis. Demographics lowered euro area r* by ~1 pp since the 1980s and are expected to lower it by a further 0.25–0.5 pp. Mechanical projections "attach a higher probability to r* staying at levels around zero, or slightly below" — [ECB OP 217](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpops/ecb.op217.en.pdf)
- Auclert, Malmberg, Martenet & Rognlie (NBER WP 29161, 2021): a sufficient-statistic approach using household surveys from 25 countries finds ageing will keep raising wealth-to-GDP, *lower asset returns* and widen global imbalances through the 21st century. This counters the Goodhart–Pradhan "ageing reverses" view — [NBER w29161](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29161)
- Inequality channel (Mian, Straub & Sufi 2020/2021, a "saving glut of the rich") is cited by Taylor (2025) as a further saving-glut force pushing r* down. The IMF WEO 2023 finds that rising inequality had a large negative impact on the US natural rate, "even more than demographic changes" — [Taylor speech](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2025/july/the-end-of-the-road-speech-by-alan-taylor.pdf); [IMF WEO 2023 Ch. 2](https://www.imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/WEO/2023/April/English/ch2.ashx)

**Post-2019/2022 rebound and "higher r*" vs "secular stagnation persists"**
- NY Fed (Del Negro, Elbarmi & Pham, Liberty Street, Feb 2026): a "trendy VAR" on 18 advanced economies, including the UK, finds:
  - Global r* fell ~3.5 pp from ~3% in the early 1990s to below 0% after the GFC.
  - From 2019 to 2024, global r* rose ~0.8 pp and US r* ~1.1 pp. The 2024 levels are global 0.31% and US 0.46%.
  - The convenience yield explains ~⅓ of both the pre-COVID decline and the post-COVID rebound. Possible AI productivity gains and expected higher debt/GDP (demographic pressures, defence spending) are cited for the rest.
  - The 68% band runs from ~−0.5% to >1%; the 95% band from ~−1.5% to >2%.
  - Source: [Liberty Street Economics, "The Post-Pandemic Global R*"](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/02/the-post-pandemic-global-r/)
- BoE Feb 2025 MPR Box A, Table 1: the BoE's qualitative ledger of drivers since 2018, built on the Cesa-Bianchi et al. (2022) framework plus Eggertsson et al. (2019), Del Negro et al. (2019) and Rachel & Summers (2019). The fuller Box A findings are in Section 3.
  - **Weighing on R*:** demographic trends (continued ageing; the 2024 UN projections show faster fertility decline), global trade fragmentation (lower productivity), higher risk and risk aversion (precautionary saving, lower investment).
  - **Pushing up R*:**
    - global financial fragmentation, which would raise AE R* and lower EM R*
    - expansionary global fiscal policy and rising debt/GDP
    - AI: Goldman Sachs estimates global trend growth could be ~1½ pp higher over a decade, while Acemoğlu & Johnson see much smaller effects
    - climate change: NGFS Phase IV analysis suggests a small net increase, but the direction is uncertain
  - Source: [BoE MPR Feb 2025, Box A](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/monetary-policy-report/2025/february-2025) ([PDF](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2025.pdf))
- Obstfeld (2023, "Natural and Neutral Real Interest Rates: Past and Future", NBER WP 31949 / 10th Asian Monetary Policy Forum):
  - Market indicators show equilibrium long-term real rates have risen recently.
  - But the main factors that pushed rates down — demographics, lower productivity growth, corporate market power, and safe-asset demand relative to supply — "do not appear poised to reverse and drive a durable rise in global real interest rates". "Low equilibrium interest rates probably will continue to bedevil monetary policy."
  - He estimates the overall fall in global real rates at roughly 450 bp.
  - Source: [ABFER PDF](https://abfer.org/media/abfer-events-2023/ampf/MauriceObstfeld_Natural-Rates-of-Interest.pdf); [NBER w31949](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/31949.html)
- Taylor (BoE MPC, "The end of the road", LSE, 4 Jul 2025): a "secular stagnation persists" view.
  - Historically, r* was propped up by five conditions: poor financial contracting, a backward financial sector, few savers, fast domestic growth needing investment, and strong foreign borrowing demand. "For the first time in history all five forces are absent."
  - Climate/AI investment needs "may not be of such a magnitude" to reverse this. A reversal of capital flows to poor countries is possible but he doubts it.
  - "21st century r* will be defined most of all by yet more ageing." He notes the Goodhart–Pradhan counter-argument.
  - Source: [BoE speech PDF](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2025/july/the-end-of-the-road-speech-by-alan-taylor.pdf)

**UK levels (structural/semi-structural)**
- Taylor (2025): updated Davis et al. (2024) macro-finance model (HLW-type macro block plus an affine term-structure block). UK r* has fallen from ~3% in the 1980s to "near 1% now". The point estimate is "about 0.75% per annum for Q1 2025", similar to 2015–16. Across the G5 (US, UK, Japan, France, Germany), 95% intervals are ~50–150 bp wide, and UK and US r* are above Japan and the euro area — [Taylor speech](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2025/july/the-end-of-the-road-speech-by-alan-taylor.pdf)
- BoE Feb 2025 Box A: the August 2018 Inflation Report put the long-run nominal equilibrium Bank Rate at 2–3%, i.e. real ~0–1%. Since then, staff macro models show a rise of ~25–75 bp — [BoE MPR Feb 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- A defensible structural anchor for UK real r* in 2024–26 is **~0.5–1.25%**: the 2018 range of 0–1% plus the 25–75 bp model rise, consistent with Taylor's 0.75% ±~0.5.
- Structural OLG models are better at explaining *trends and scenarios* than at nowcasting the current level. They suit a dashboard's "drivers" panel (e.g. a waterfall of demographics, productivity, fiscal, safe-asset effects) better than its point estimate.
- The "higher r*" forces (fiscal, convenience-yield erosion, AI, financial fragmentation) are each tens of basis points in IMF-type simulations, not percentage points. Ageing is a persistent drag. Upside risks are therefore mostly scenario tails, not the baseline.

### Gaps
- Gourinchas & Rey (and Gourinchas, Rey & Sauzet 2022), Eggertsson, Mehrotra & Robbins (2019) and Mian–Straub–Sufi: I did not verify specific numerical results from primary sources. The BoE (Box A) and Taylor cite them only qualitatively.
- IMF WEO 2023 UK-specific projected path: it is in Online Annex 2 / Figure 2.8, but I could not extract UK numbers from the chart.
- Kiley (2020) on the debt → r* coefficient: no specific bp-per-pp-of-debt figure found. The IMF WEO gives +5–10 bp for +25 pp of debt/GDP. Rachel & Summers (2019) and other literature give larger effects, but I did not verify their exact figures.
- Platzer, Grätz et al. IMF 2022 "global semi-structural r*": not located. Only Platzer & Peruffo (US) was verified.

## 2. How does UK r* relate to global r* in an open economy?

### Takeaway
BoE official doctrine (Feb 2025 MPR) is that for a small open economy like the UK, long-run R* "will ultimately be determined by global forces". In practice, empirical work finds a persistent country-specific wedge. Global/US shocks explain only about a quarter to a third of the UK's post-2007 r* decline, and the UK sits above Japan and the euro area. The best model is therefore **UK r* ≈ global r* + a time-varying UK-specific component**, with high uncertainty.

### Cited Findings
- BoE Feb 2025 MPR Box A: "For a small open economy like the UK, R* will ultimately be determined by global forces… frictions that impede the free international movement of capital are weaker over the long run, and free capital movement will tend to equalise capital returns across countries (Bailey et al (2022))." At the global level, R* balances desired savings and desired capital — [BoE MPR Feb 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2025.pdf)
- Bailey (2022 speech, "The economic landscape: structural change, global R* and the missing-investment puzzle") is the underlying BoE framing, citing Cesa-Bianchi et al. — [BIS review](https://bis.org/review/r220713d.htm); see also [BoE SWP 997](https://ideas.repec.org/p/boe/boeewp/0997.html)
- Taylor (2025), footnote 8: "Without financial frictions and with free flow of capital, one would expect neutral rates to converge across countries. However, these frictions exist, and they cause persistent cross-country gaps between neutral rates." He cites Cesa-Bianchi, Lloyd, Sajedi & Sampaolesi (forthcoming), "The Natural Rate of Interest in Small-Open Economies: Asymmetries and Fragmentation" — [Taylor speech](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2025/july/the-end-of-the-road-speech-by-alan-taylor.pdf)
- Morley & Wong (2025, "How important is global r-star for open economies?", RBA workshop):
  - Covers Australia, Canada, the euro area, NZ, Norway, Sweden and the UK.
  - (i) Shocks driving US r* are "almost entirely sufficient" to capture global r*. (ii) Local shocks are also important. (iii) Global forces played the leading role in the post-GFC decline.
  - For the **UK**, "domestic shocks account for a large share of the decline, [but] over a quarter to a third of their decline in r* can still be accounted for by global shocks" (Brexit falls within the sample). UK estimates carry more estimation uncertainty than those for Canada or Sweden.
  - Source: [RBA workshop PDF](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/workshops/research/2025/pdf/rba-workshop-2025-morley-wong.pdf)
- NY Fed (Del Negro et al., Feb 2026): cross-country dispersion in r* among 18 advanced economies, including the UK, "disappears all of a sudden" after the late 1980s, "arguably as a result of financial market integration". The r* trend is now genuinely global. Global r* in 2024 was 0.31% — [Liberty Street Economics](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/02/the-post-pandemic-global-r/)
- IMF WEO 2023: international spillovers are "significant" but work through two offsetting channels. EM growth raised AE rates, while the EM saving glut and safe-asset demand lowered them. The net effect on natural rates was "moderate". Projections assume some AE–EM capital-market segmentation persists (citing Obstfeld 2021) — [IMF WEO Apr 2023 Ch. 2](https://www.imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/WEO/2023/April/English/ch2.ashx)
- Obstfeld (2023): with global determination of real rates, "measurement methodologies based on closed-economy models may mislead". In an open economy, saving need not equal investment. External financial shocks (capital-flow surges) can move the domestic natural rate and the policy-relevant neutral rate in *different* directions — [Obstfeld 2023](https://abfer.org/media/abfer-events-2023/ampf/MauriceObstfeld_Natural-Rates-of-Interest.pdf)
- Bank of Canada practice: "the US neutral rate serves as a proxy for the global neutral rate" in the OLG model. Canadian-specific factors (population growth, productivity, longevity, debt, inequality) then shift Canada's rate relative to it. For 2025, both US and Canada were assessed at 2.25–3.25% nominal — [BoC SAN 2025-16](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2025/06/staff-analytical-note-2025-16/)
- BoE Aug 2025 MPR Box A: UK gilt yields are heavily influenced by international developments (citing Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2021 and Mann 2022) — [BoE MPR Aug 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/august/monetary-policy-report-august-2025.pdf)
- Williams (NY Fed): the GDP-weighted average HLW r* for Canada, the euro area, the UK and the US is "around half a percent", versus about 1% for the US. The NY Fed HLW page currently publishes US, Canada and euro area only; the UK is not in the current HLW release — [NY Fed r* page](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/policy/rstar); [Williams slides Aug 2025](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/newsevents/speeches/2025/williams-slides-250825.pdf) (note: this characterisation is from a search-engine summary. I did not verify the exact wording in the slides.)

### Inferences
- For a dashboard: show a **global r*** line (e.g. the NY Fed trendy-VAR global 0.3% for 2024, the Cesa-Bianchi et al. structural Global R*, HLW GDP-weighted ~0.5%) next to UK-specific estimates. Present the gap as the "UK premium".
- Current evidence suggests UK r* sits **around 0.25–0.5 pp above global/euro-area r***. Taylor's UK 0.75% vs NY Fed global ~0.3% gives ~+0.4 pp. The ECB's euro area range is −0.5 to +0.5%. This is an inference from different models, not a published "premium" estimate.
- The BoC's "US ≈ global, then adjust for domestic factors" is a transparent, replicable template that the UK dashboard could copy.

### Gaps
- The Cesa-Bianchi, Lloyd, Sajedi & Sampaolesi paper on small-open-economy r* asymmetries was "forthcoming/mimeo" at July 2025. I found no published UK-premium number.
- Broadbent (2023), Greene (2023), Lombardelli (2025/26) and Bailey speeches specifically on UK vs global r*: none found with explicit r* numbers. Greene's June 2026 speech is about energy-shock inflation, not r*. Lombardelli's Sep 2026 speech is on wages and inflation.
- The Mann (May 2025) slides were retrieved, but they present illustrative "short-term vs long-term neutral" term-structure charts. I extracted no numerical UK r* estimates or global comparison from them. Her preferred concept is "the short-term rate at which the economy is neither stimulated nor contracted within the monetary policy horizon" — [Mann slides](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2025/may/the-neutral-rate-of-interest-and-its-relevance-for-monetary-policy-slides-by-catherine-l-mann.pdf)

## 3. How do central banks combine models (suites, averaging, thick modelling, presenting ranges)? And what are the current UK estimates for 2024–2026?

### Takeaway
No major central bank publishes a single official r* number. The norm is a **range across model classes**: semi-structural/HLW, macro-finance/term-structure, structural OLG/DSGE, surveys, and market-implied. Each estimate is shown with its own uncertainty band, often next to a simple average. Specific practices:

| Bank | Practice |
|---|---|
| BoE | Bars by method class |
| ECB | Min–max range across four model families |
| BoC | Range from counterfactual inputs across three models, rounded to 25 bp |
| Norges Bank | Model-portfolio average plus interval |
| RBA | Average of models, with explicit caveats |
| Fed | FOMC SEP longer-run median as a de facto "official" number, beside a public suite of model estimates |

UK real r* estimates for 2024–26 cluster at **~0.5–1.25% (models)** and **~1–1.5% (market/survey)**, i.e. roughly **2.5–3.5% nominal**.

### Cited Findings

**Bank of England**
- **Feb 2025 MPR Box A (long-run R*)**: presents "a range of estimates" from three empirical approaches since 2018. It publishes no single number, and R* "is not used by the MPC as a direct guide to setting policy".
  - (a) Staff macro models: +25 to +75 bp vs the Aug 2018 IR. The Davis et al. (2024) two-trend macro-finance model gives ~+25 bp; the Del Negro et al. (2019)-based semi-structural model gives ~+75 bp.
  - (b) Market-based measures (10-year UK forward real rates from nominal gilts deflated by survey inflation expectations): up by over 90 bp since 2018. These may overstate the rise because of term premia and the oversensitivity of long yields.
  - (c) Surveys: the MaPS neutral Bank Rate is up 150 bp "in recent years". Consensus 6–10-year-ahead 10-year yields minus inflation imply the UK long-term real rate is up by less than 25 bp since 2018.
  - Conclusion: R* "likely to have increased modestly… significant uncertainty around the range of estimates at any point", and Bank Rate "unlikely to fall back to its pre-pandemic lows". Baseline: the Aug 2018 range of 2–3% nominal long-run.
  - Source: [BoE MPR Feb 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/monetary-policy-report/2025/february-2025); [PDF](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2025.pdf)
- **Aug 2025 MPR Box A (shorter-run r* / real-rate gaps)**: the BoE presents *real-rate gaps* (expected real policy rate over three years minus r*) as ranges by method class. The classes are:
  - (1) Macro models: a semi-structural IS-curve model (Kalman filter, 1988Q3–2025Q2, ranges from a dynamic factor model) and one of the main forecast models (Albuquerque et al. 2025).
  - (2) A macro-financial model: Davis et al. (2024) with a 95% band around central r*.
  - (3) Financial markets: nominal neutral from three term-premia models (Malik & Meldrum 2016; Vlieghe 2016; Meldrum & Roberts-Sklar 2015), deflated by Consensus 6–10-year inflation expectations.
  - (4) MaPS 10th–90th percentile of respondent-implied gaps.
  - The BoE stresses that restrictiveness is "highly dependent on the level of r* embodied" and on the inflation-expectations measure, and recommends *nominal* gaps as a cross-check. Market measures reflect long-run R* more than short-run r*.
  - Source: [BoE MPR Aug 2025](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2025/august/monetary-policy-report-august-2025.pdf)
- **Feb 2026 MPR Section 3**: the updated models show "a small positive real interest rate gap, ranging from around 0–1 percentage point" (macro models and the Davis et al. macro-financial model). The market-term-premia range now uses *five* models (Malik & Meldrum benchmark and survey versions, Vlieghe, Andreasen & Meldrum 2015, Meldrum & Roberts-Sklar). MaPS and the DMP survey (10th–90th percentile) are also shown. Those ranges "are wide enough that it is possible that Bank Rate may be around neutral or slightly accommodative". Bank Rate was 3.75% at the time — [BoE MPR Feb 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/monetary-policy-report/2026/february-2026) ([PDF](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/monetary-policy-report/2026/february/monetary-policy-report-february-2026.pdf))
- Taylor (2025): the central UK r* is 0.75% (Q1 2025). Adding 2% gives 2.75% nominal neutral, with a range of **2.25–3.25%** from the ~100 bp-wide 95% posterior interval. He calls it not "a particularly controversial or unusual range", and argued Bank Rate (then 4.25%) had "a long way to go to get to neutral" — [Taylor speech](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/speech/2025/july/the-end-of-the-road-speech-by-alan-taylor.pdf)
- BoE Market Participants Survey, Sep 2026: "level of Bank Rate at which monetary policy is neither expansionary nor contractionary": **median 3.25% (25th–75th: 3.00–3.50%)**. Expected Bank Rate five years ahead (Sep 2031): median 3.25% (3.25–3.50%) — [BoE MaPS Sep 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/markets/market-intelligence/survey-results/2026/market-participants-survey-results-september-2026). The March 2026 MaPS also reported a median of 3.25% (3.00–3.50%), per a secondary aggregator — [Better Regulation](https://service.betterregulation.com/document/839413)

**ECB**
- ECB Economic Bulletin 1/2025 box ("Natural rate estimates for the euro area: insights, uncertainties and shortcomings"):
  - Four model classes: survey-based, term-structure, semi-structural, and HLW.
  - For Q4 2024, real r* ranges from **−0.5% to +0.5%**, i.e. **1.75–2.25% nominal**. Including HLW (to Q3 2024) widens this to real −0.5% to +1% and nominal 1.75–3%.
  - Three layers of uncertainty are named: model, parameter and filtering. Parameter plus filter uncertainty "can span up to several percentage points" for a single model, and HLW revisions have been up to 1 pp between quarters.
  - Estimates are not "a mechanical gauge of appropriate monetary policy".
  - Source: [ECB EB Box 2025/1](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/economic-bulletin/focus/2025/html/ecb.ebbox202501_08~3be5a005f9.en.html)
- ECB OP 217 (2018): presented each model with its own 90% error band side by side (Fiorentini et al., Brand & Mazelis, Jarociński, Ajevskis, Haavio et al., Gerali & Neri) rather than pooling. It stresses "high degree of uncertainty surrounding mid-point estimates" and "model dependence" — [ECB OP 217](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpops/ecb.op217.en.pdf)

**Bank of Canada**
- Annual assessment (Staff Analytical Note / Paper, with an MPR appendix). Three models:
  - an OLG model (productivity, demographics, government debt, inequality; the US serves as a proxy for global)
  - a risk-augmented neoclassical growth model (tail-risk channel)
  - a two-country term-structure model (market information)
- The range is built from "different counterfactuals with respect to key inputs", reflects outputs across all three models "rather than a simple average", and is rounded to the nearest 25 bp.
- Canada **2.25–3.25% nominal** (2024, 2025 and 2026, unchanged; midpoint 2.75% ⇒ real ~0.75%). US: 2.25–3.25% in 2025, raised to **2.50–3.50%** in 2026 on stronger potential growth.
- Sources: [BoC SAN 2025-16](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2025/06/staff-analytical-note-2025-16/); [BoC SAP 2026-21](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2026/05/staff-analytical-paper-2026-21/); [MPR Apr 2026 appendix](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/publications/mpr/mpr-2026-04-29/appendix/)

**Federal Reserve**
- The FOMC SEP median longer-run fed funds rate was **3.1%** at the March 2026 meeting, implying real r* ≈ 1.1%. A St Louis Fed comparison (May 2026) builds an "alternative r*" as the **geometric mean of six measures**: HLW, LW, Lubik–Matthes, D'Amico–Kim–Wei, Zaman, and the market 10y10y TIPS. That alternative was **1.43%** at Q4 2025, with HLW ~1.0% (lowest) and 10y10y TIPS slightly above 3% (highest). The SEP "tends to adjust toward the alternative r-star estimate, albeit with a lag" — [St Louis Fed, May 2026](https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2026/may/comparing-fomc-estimate-r-star-alternative-estimates)
- The NY Fed publishes HLW and LW estimates quarterly with explicit uncertainty caveats — [NY Fed r*](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/policy/rstar)

**Norges Bank**
- Uses a "model portfolio" and publishes the **average point estimate**. In 2025 that was **0.4% real**, with two additional finance-rich models added and "a gradual upward trend… post-pandemic" — [Norges Bank Staff Memo 8/2025](https://www.norges-bank.no/en/news-events/publications/Staff-Memo/2025/staff-memo-8-2025/)
- The MPR states the neutral real rate lies in a 0–1% interval, per search summary — [Norges Bank MPR 1/2025](https://www.norges-bank.no/en/news-events/publications/Monetary-Policy-Report/2025/mpr-12025/web-report-mpr-12025/) (not verified in full text)

**RBA**
- Uses the "average of a range of alternative model estimates". The RBA warns that estimates "are subject to considerable dispersion and uncertainty and the average is sensitive to the choice of models included". Minutes say public discussion "possibly overemphasised the inferences" from these models, especially for the near term. The August 2026 SMP describes the cash rate as "at the top of the range of central estimates of neutral both from the RBA's models and from market economists" — [RBA Board minutes Jul 2025](https://www.rba.gov.au/monetary-policy/rba-board-minutes/2025/2025-07-08.html); [RBA SMP Aug 2024](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/smp/2024/aug/financial-conditions.html); [RBA SMP Aug 2026](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/smp/2026/aug/financial-conditions.html)

### Inferences
Best practice for presenting a credible dashboard range:
- **(a)** Group estimates by *method class* (semi-structural/HLW, macro-finance, structural/OLG, market-implied, surveys), as the BoE, ECB and BoC do. Don't pool them all into one average.
- **(b)** Show each estimate with its own uncertainty band. Separately show the cross-model envelope (min–max of central estimates), which represents model uncertainty.
- **(c)** If a single headline is wanted, follow Norges Bank or St Louis Fed: a simple or geometric mean, labelled as an average, with a note that it is sensitive to model choice (the RBA caveat).
- **(d)** Round ranges to 25 bp and report real and nominal (+2%) side by side (BoC, ECB, Taylor).
- **(e)** Keep long-run R* (structural, market 5y5y/10y) visibly separate from shorter-run r* (the policy-horizon neutral). The BoE's Aug 2025 Box A and Mann (2025) both stress that the two differ.
- **(f)** Add a nominal-gap cross-check, because real gaps are sensitive to the inflation-expectations deflator.

A dashboard UK real r* central range of **~0.5–1.25% (≈2.5–3.25% nominal)** would sit comfortably inside the BoE, Taylor, BoC-analogue and MaPS evidence. Market/survey measures sit at the upper end (~1.25%+), and the global structural estimates at the lower end (~0.3–0.5%).

- The BoE Feb 2025 Box A chart levels (not just changes since 2018) for each method were not extractable from text. Only the changes are stated in the prose.
- The Brand, Lisack & Mazelis (2024) ECB update was not retrieved directly. The ECB EB 2025/1 box is used as the latest ECB suite.
- Formal "thick modelling" or Bayesian model averaging for r* in central-bank publications: I found no central bank using formal BMA or density-pooling weights publicly. Norges Bank (simple average) and St Louis Fed (geometric mean) are the only explicit aggregation rules found.
